I am a postdoctoral fellow at the [Center for Theoretical Physics – a Leinweber Institute](https://ctp.lns.mit.edu/) at MIT, where I work with [Mikhail Ivanov](https://physics.mit.edu/faculty/mikhail-ivanov/), and a Junior Investigator at the [NSF AI Institute for Artificial Intelligence and Fundamental Interactions](https://iaifi.org/). Before coming to MIT I was a Cosmology Fellow at [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://www.lbl.gov/) and the [Berkeley Center for Cosmological Physics](https://bccp.berkeley.edu/) at UC Berkeley, working with [Simone Ferraro](https://sferraro.lbl.gov/) and [Nathalie Palanque-Delabrouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathalie_Palanque-Delabrouille). I did my PhD at the University of Cambridge with [George Efstathiou](https://people.ast.cam.ac.uk/~gpe/), after studying physics at ETH Zurich and writing my master's thesis at Stanford with [Leonardo Senatore](https://www.phys.ethz.ch/the-department/people/person-detail.MjgyMzgw.TGlzdC84MzgsMTE3MjU5OTI5OQ==.html).

**Cosmology.** I study the distant Universe to learn about its basic ingredients: how much neutrinos weigh, what drives the accelerating expansion, and how the first structures formed. My main tool is the Lyman-α forest, the pattern of absorption lines that hydrogen gas between the galaxies imprints on the light of distant quasars. I build theoretical models and machine-learning methods for this signal and apply them to data from the [Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)](https://www.desi.lbl.gov/), where I lead the Lyman-α forest theory task force, and from the Planck satellite. Our recent paper on modelling the forest was selected as a Featured article in *Physical Review Letters* and highlighted in [*Physics*](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v19/s33).

**Physics and AI.** I am equally interested in how artificial intelligence and science can advance each other. AI can sharpen the traditional steps of research, such as simulation, modelling and statistical inference, but it can also open new ways of doing science: generating and testing models, scaling inference to problems that were out of reach, and probing how far automated scientific reasoning can go. Physics is a good place to develop such methods: it offers a well-defined sandbox spanning theory, computation and experiment, and what works there often carries over to the other sciences. I also care about the wider questions this raises: how new technology changes what research is possible, how rapidly improving AI is reshaping scientific practice, and how academic and industry research relate to one another. In 2026 this work was recognised with an Anthropic AI for Science award.

I am always happy to hear from students and potential collaborators.
